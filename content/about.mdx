# About

I'm Harsh, a CSE student from India. I'm in the final year of my B.Tech at
Sharda University, specialising in AI and ML, and I graduate in June 2027.

## What I build

Most of my projects start because something annoyed me.

When I started learning Japanese, I went through random YouTube videos,
Duolingo and a handful of other sites, and every one of them drew the
characters a little differently: dots on one, strokes on another, for kana like
shi and tsu. That's fine if you already know the language. It wasn't fine for
me. So I made [Nihongo Path](https://nihongo.harshnarain.me), the course I
actually wanted: kana up to JLPT N4, pronunciation written in Devanagari rather
than romaji, and a review system that brings a word back just before you'd
forget it. It's for Hindi speakers, which is a niche I happen to be in. I still
revise on it every day.

This summer I interned at Siddhi Sarees, on the team rebuilding their website.
My actual job was small fixes. What I couldn't stop looking at was the orders:
they moved through personal WhatsApp chats, phone calls, pen and paper and the
odd sticky note, and the only people who could tell you where one stood were
the ones making bills at the front desk. I felt overwhelmed just imagining
being in their place, so I built an
[order dashboard](https://siddhi.harshnarain.me) that nobody had asked for.

Looking back, it's a prototype (a glorified Excel sheet, if I'm honest): no
login, no database, no way to edit an order from the dashboard itself. The
timeline and the metrics were the genuinely useful part, and also the part
that's hardest to make work in a real shop.

There's also [Budget Buddy](https://budget.harshnarain.me), which shows me
where my money actually went, and
[ShareTheMeal](https://sharethemeal.harshnarain.me), a small app for posting
leftover food for NGOs nearby (it runs in demo mode, so the NGOs are made up).

## How I work now

For a long time I built things until they worked and stopped there. Now I
check load times and image sizes, use SVGs wherever I can, and write comments
and commits that someone new could follow at a glance. I picked that up at the
internship, from a codebase full of comments people had written by hand, long
before AI tools were around. You could understand a feature without asking
anyone; I want my own repos to read like that (they're getting there).

I'd also rather read the documentation than follow a tutorial made for
everyone. A tutorial teaches you its project. The docs teach you the tool.

## Research

I also do a bit of ML research, and both projects are about numbers that look
fine when they shouldn't. One catches bugs in training code that break the
gradients but leave test accuracy looking normal. The other found that most
test cases in a popular rare-disease benchmark come from the same papers as the
answers they're graded against, which makes methods look better than they
are. The first one ran mostly overnight on my laptop's RTX 3050.
