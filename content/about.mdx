## Research

Most of my time goes into two research projects. Both have been submitted to
conferences, and both taught me more by failing than they would have by working.

The first is **RareSight**. It takes a patient's symptoms, written as
standardised phenotype codes, and tries to rank which of 557 rare diseases they
might have. I built a graph neural network for it, and the graph network lost
badly to a much simpler method that just compares symptom lists directly. Rather
than swap in the thing that won and move on, I spent the rest of the project
working out why, and ended up proving that a model trained on the same ontology
it's tested against can't beat that ontology used directly. Along the way I found
that the benchmark everyone uses quietly rewards models for repeating the
patient's own words back at them. That turned into the paper's main argument.

The second asks whether you can tell when your training code is silently wrong.
I broke the backward pass on purpose in one specific layer, then ran 7,070
training runs across five datasets to see whether the damage showed up in how
the model behaved. Past about two layers deep, it doesn't. The network absorbs
the fault and trains to nearly the same accuracy, which is an unsettling thing to
learn about code you'd otherwise trust. All of it ran on one RTX 3050, mostly
overnight.
